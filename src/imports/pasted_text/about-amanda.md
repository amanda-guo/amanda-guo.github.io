### Hi, I'm Amanda.

**I work at the intersection of product, technology and people.**

Which is a slightly more polished way of saying that I've spent the last few years trying to figure out exactly where I fit.

I've been a software engineer, designer, consultant and technology educator. I've worked with product teams, engineers, designers, business leaders and learners.

And somewhere along the way, I realized that the thing I enjoy most is **connecting perspectives that don't always speak the same language.**

↓ **Come see how I got here**

---

# I didn't really know what I wanted to do.

Choosing to study Computer Science was a bit of a random act for me.

Truthfully, my grade 12 self did not know what I wanted to do for a career. And how could I at the ripe age of 18? I barely experienced the world.

So after submitting applications to any and all university programs that I thought were plausible paths, I decided to pursue Computer Science because I felt like it would open some doors.

It did.

I just didn't expect to walk through quite so many of them.

---

# First, I learned how to build things.

My first internship was as a software engineer at CIBC.

I worked on a legacy application that helped financial advisors work with high-net-worth clients on things like education and retirement planning.

And when I say legacy, I mean *legacy*.

This application looked like it was made in 2002 during the Windows XP era. And truthfully, that is probably how old it was.

I learned a lot about software, databases and the complicated world of financial technology.

But I also noticed something.

**We were all tech, and no people.**

I knew what I was building.

I didn't really know **why** I was building it, or who was actually on the other side of the screen.

Who were these mysterious "financial advisors"?

Why were we migrating the system?

And were the financial advisors as bothered by the Windows XP experience as I was?

Naturally, these questions led me somewhere else.

---

# I started looking for the people.

I moved into product and design roles at Tecsys, WSIB and Arctic Wolf.

At Tecsys, I was the first "official" product designer on my team. I learned how to listen to users, extract pain points from interviews and video footage, and translate what I heard into better experiences.

At WSIB, I got to take that process end-to-end.

We were designing a COVID-19 self-assessment application to help workers return to the office safely. Suddenly things like trust, accessibility, information hierarchy and even **whether someone could comfortably use the application from six feet away** became design problems.

I was starting to understand what it meant to design for a person, rather than just build for a system.

---

# Then I realized design had constraints too.

At Arctic Wolf, I worked on simplifying a configuration-editing workflow for security engineers.

The existing process involved remotely logging into an environment, editing JSON, pushing code to GitHub, going through code review, merging changes and waiting for them to propagate.

It could take half a day.

The obvious answer was: **give people a UI.**

The less obvious answer was: *okay, but what kind of UI can we actually build within the technical constraints we already have?*

This was where my engineering background became an advantage.

I could understand the technical limitations while still advocating for the person using the system.

I learned that the first version doesn't have to be perfect.

Sometimes V1 just needs to be **workable**.

That experience made me realize that design and engineering weren't really separate worlds.

I wanted to understand both.

---

# So I zoomed out.

At Deloitte, I worked on a six-month migration of a legacy application to a cross-platform Flutter application, coordinating across product, design and engineering.

For the first time, I got to see product development from an executive lens.

I started thinking about questions that had nothing to do with the implementation details:

**What value are we creating?**

**What does success look like?**

**Why are we doing this in the first place?**

I learned that business strategy can have a huge effect on execution.

And I started to see the role I naturally played in these environments: helping different teams understand one another and turn a high-level direction into something people could actually execute.

---

# And then I had a bit of an identity crisis.

By this point, I'd dabbled in a variety of different roles in technology.

I had gathered a breadth of experience that I truthfully had no idea what to do with.

So I started asking myself:

> **Do I become an engineer?**
> 
> 
> **A product manager?**
> 
> **A product designer?**
> 
> **A technology consultant?**
> 

I had no idea.

But when I looked back at everything I'd done, I noticed something.

**The roles were different. The pattern wasn't.**

I kept finding myself somewhere between disciplines.

Between engineering and design.

Between product and the business.

Between technical teams and the people they were building for.

I was starting to realize that maybe I didn't need to choose one perspective.

Maybe the value was in being able to understand several.

---

# So I went back to engineering.

I joined Wealthsimple as a software engineer and got to experience what it was like to work in a fast-paced fintech startup where code shipped to production every week.

I built real-time streaming quotes for options trading and worked across mobile and web experiences.

But I wasn't the same engineer I would have been a few years earlier.

I brought my design and product perspective with me.

I cared about the implementation, but I also cared about how the implementation felt.

I wanted the experience to make sense to the person using it, not just the system underneath it.

And that became a recurring theme in my work.

---

# Then I started crossing the boundary.

At Manulife, I joined as a software engineer and expanded from frontend into full-stack development.

One of the applications I worked on became known internally as the **"pizza tracker."**

It was inspired by Domino's pizza tracker — except instead of tracking your pepperoni pizza, it helped insurance and investment advisors track the status of claims they had submitted on behalf of their clients.

The problem was surprisingly similar:

**people just wanted to know where their thing was.**

I started out building the application.

But over time, I became involved in business and technical requirement scoping, architecture decisions and design collaboration.

I was still an engineer.

But my title didn't really describe everything I was doing.

**I was naturally working beyond the boundaries of the role.**

---

# And then I started working horizontally.

My most recent role at Manulife brought me into Manulife University, where I work on training and upskilling technologists across the organization in new technologies, tools and AI.

This role is very different from building a product for one team.

Some weeks I'm developing learning content.

Some weeks I'm coaching instructor-led sessions.

Some weeks I'm working with senior leaders to figure out what their teams need to learn.

Some weeks I'm troubleshooting a hands-on lab five minutes before someone is supposed to use it.

Every week is a little different.

And, somehow, all of the perspectives I've collected along the way became useful.

I can understand the technology.

I can think about the person learning it.

I can understand what the organization is trying to accomplish.

And I can help turn a fairly ambiguous idea into something tangible.

---

# Looking back, it wasn't actually that random.

On paper, my career probably looks a little all over the place.

**Software engineering → design → consulting → engineering → technology enablement → AI.**

For a while, I had a hard time connecting the dots myself.

But I think I've finally figured out what the thread is.

I wasn't really changing directions.

**I was collecting perspectives.**

Design taught me to understand people.

Engineering taught me how things actually get built.

Product taught me to think about what should be built and why.

Consulting taught me to zoom out and think about business value.

Technology enablement taught me how organizations learn and adopt new ideas.

And working across all of them taught me how to **connect perspectives and turn ideas into action.**

---

# So, what do I actually do?

I work at the intersection of **product, technology and people.**

My background spans software engineering, design, product, consulting and technology enablement.

Working across these disciplines has taught me how to navigate ambiguity, communicate across different perspectives and build things that work — for the people using them, the teams building them and the organizations behind them.

I don't think I have one neatly defined box.

And, honestly, I'm increasingly okay with that.

---

# What's next?

Apparently, **AI.**

I'm interested in what happens when the tools we use to build technology start changing how we think about building it in the first place.

I'm learning, experimenting, building things, and trying not to get too distracted by the approximately 47 other things I could be learning at the same time.

We'll see where that goes.
